Case: '482 patent — {{party_a}} (patent holder) vs {{party_b}} (licensee/defendant)

--------------------------------------------------------------
SHARED FACTS — available to both parties
--------------------------------------------------------------

SETTING (2010)

It is 2010. {{party_b}} is preparing to release a new smartphone with a built-in camera and multimedia messaging service (MMS) functionality. MMS allows users to send photos via text messages.

The parties agree that the '482 patent is valid and infringed by {{party_b}} smartphones that include the MMS photo-upload/sharing feature.

{{party_b}} BUSINESS CONTEXT

- Average selling price per phone: $228.23
- Profit margin: 19.11% (approx. $43.61 in profit per phone)
- Projected smartphone sales during the patent term: 103.6 million units

CAMERA / MMS CONTRIBUTION

- {{party_b}} cost data, disclosed for these negotiations: camera accounts for 6.2% of phone production cost.
- Industry surveys:
  - 65% of users use the phone's camera for photos (not video)
  - Of those, 77% share their photos
  - Of those, 41% use MMS rather than email or web
- Together: 20.5% of users use the infringing MMS feature.

COMPARABLE LICENSES

- Facebook paid $20 million for a non-exclusive, fully paid-up license to the '482 patent.
- BlackBerry paid $3 million for a similar license (still a strong communications player in 2010, position eroding).
- eBay paid $3 million for a full image processing suite.
- iPIX paid $735,000 for a license (smaller image-processing / virtual-tour company).

INDUSTRY CONTEXT

- Industry estimates put over 250,000 active patents on smartphone technologies.
- Users select smartphones based on many factors (screen size, apps, hardware), not just MMS.

VALUATION APPROACH

- A per-unit royalty can be derived as: profit per phone ($43.61) × camera cost share (6.2%) × infringing-feature usage (20.5%) × the share of that value allocated to the patent holder. Multiply by projected unit sales for a lump-sum equivalent.
- Either side may use this approach, adjust it, or argue for a different one entirely.

IF NEGOTIATIONS FAIL

- The dispute proceeds to federal court litigation. The '482 patent is expected to be found valid and infringed, so the uncertainty is over remedies only.
- Probability of injunction: 80%. {{party_a}} is a small technology company that competes directly with {{party_b}} in the mobile device market, so under eBay Inc. v. MercExchange an injunction is likely.
- Consequence of an injunction: {{party_b}} must stop selling infringing handsets until MMS photo-sharing is removed. Redesign, recertification, and the resulting launch delay put an estimated 12 million units of projected sales at risk.
- Damages exposure: {{party_b}} would face a reasonable-royalty award based on what a jury believes would have resulted from a successful hypothetical negotiation.

--------------------------------------------------------------
CONFIDENTIAL — {{party_a}} ONLY. Do not disclose to {{party_b}}.
--------------------------------------------------------------

POSITION

- The patent is valid and infringed. Keep negotiations focused on the lump-sum or royalty amount and timing, not on liability.
- You compete directly with {{party_b}} in mobile devices. An injunction would not just cost them money, it would take a competing product off the market. How much leverage that gives you depends on how likely an injunction is.
- Your litigation costs are estimated at $3 million in attorney's fees, excluding appeals.

OBJECTIVES

- No target settlement figure is given to you. Work out your own minimum from your litigation alternative: your attorney's fees, the probability of an injunction, and what an injunction would be worth to you.
- Litigation wastes money for both sides, but any settlement should still capture fair value for MMS photo-sharing across the projected 103.6M units.
- Do not accept a nominal settlement that ignores the 20.5% usage rate and the profit earned on infringing phones.

TACTICS

- Injunction risk is the dominant variable in your position. The higher the probability of an injunction, the stronger your leverage and the firmer you should hold; the lower it is, the more ground you should be willing to give to close.
- Press {{party_b}} on why a negotiated licence serves them better than litigation: an injunction would halt sales of infringing handsets, and their own litigation costs and launch delay compound that exposure.
